You have three logbooks, four hours of lamp oil, and one forgery. By sunrise, you will know who forged it, and why.

The supply boat drops you on Gull Rock at six in the evening, just as the fog rolls in like wet wool. You are a relief keeper. Your training was on modern lights, the kind that switch themselves on. This one is older than your grandparents. Its keeper, Amos Kettle, seventy-nine, has not answered the radio since lunch.

The door opens before you knock. A girl of twelve, freckles, yellow raincoat, a lantern held in both hands. "I'm Maisie," she said. "Grandad rowed out at four to fix the reef bell. Then the fog came. He didn't come back."

Behind you, the supply boat is already a grey shape, then nothing. The radio crackles once. "Storm closes the harbour tonight," a tired voice says. "We come at dawn. Not before."

Here is your clock. The electric lamp died with the generator. The backup is an old oil burner, and its tank holds four hours at full flame. The night ferry passes the reef at eleven, five hours from now. Dawn is at seven. And somewhere in the fog is an old man in a small wooden boat.

You climb a hundred and twelve steps. Maisie talks the whole way up. "The burner is slow," she said. "Twenty minutes from a glow to a real beam. Grandad says wind is what eats a flame, not time."

You strike a match. The wick catches. Slowly, a gold glow swells into a beam, and the beam swings out across the water. Maisie cheers. It feels like winning.

Then you hear it. A thin whistle. One pane of the lantern room is cracked, and the west wind is pushing straight through. The flame leans and roars. You check the gauge. It has dropped like a stone. At this rate, oil for two and a half hours. The ferry is still five hours away.

Freeze. Option one, burn full flame anyway, because the ferry needs the brightest light you have. Option two, plug the cracked pane with Amos's heavy oilskin coat and turn the wick down to half. Option three, put the lamp out completely and relight it the moment you hear the ferry's horn.

Pause the video. Decide now. Your oil keeps burning.

You choose option two. The coat smells of salt and pipe smoke. The whistle stops. The flame stands up straight and calm, and the gauge finally settles. Oil for four and a half hours. Just enough, if nothing goes wrong. Full flame in that wind would have run dry before nine. And the horn plan? Remember Maisie on the stairs. Twenty minutes from glow to beam. The ferry would be on the rocks before your light woke up.

You sit on the cold iron floor. Then Maisie lifts her head.

"Listen," she said.

Nothing. No sound at all.

The reef bell has stopped. "It never stops," she whispers. "Not in a storm. Not ever."

Seven thirty. Oil for under four hours. You need to know where Amos is. Maisie pulls three logbooks from his desk. A green one, the official log, neat blue ink, every day signed "All well, A. Kettle." A red one, his own diary, written in huge, shaky pencil letters. And a black one, cracked leather, his father's book from long ago, full of drawings of the reef and the caves.

The red diary, today: "Bell clapper loose. Fix at low water." Below it, a tide note. "High water eight. North reef drowns."

On the gallery rail sits a hand signal lamp with a green glass filter. It throws a narrow beam. You can search one direction. Only one.

Freeze. Option one, the north reef, because that is where the bell is and where he said he would go. Option two, the seal cave on the east side, out of the wind. Option three, the south slipway, because surely he would try to row home.

Pause the video. Decide now. The tide keeps rising.

You choose option two. The wind is from the west. The cracked pane taught you that. A man in a small boat hides from it on the east side. The north reef drowns at eight. And rowing home against that wind, blind in this fog, is something a fifty-year keeper would never do.

You aim the lamp at the east cliffs and flash. Once. Twice.

Darkness. Then, small and stubborn, a torch blinks back. Three times.

Maisie grips the rail and laughs and cries a little, all at the same time.

If you found him, you think like a keeper. If you didn't, stay with us, because the hardest freeze hasn't happened yet. Subscribe so this light keeps finding you.

You go back to the desk to see what the black book says about the seal cave. But something bothers you. The green log. Neat ink. A steady hand. Every single day, "All well."

Amos writes in huge, shaky pencil. Every page of the red diary proves it.

"Maisie," you say. "Who writes the green log?"

She looks at the floor for a long time. "Me," she said. "Since spring. His hands got bad. If the mainland knew, they'd move him to a home in town. He'd hate it. So I copy his name. And I keep the light."

You thought you came to help an old keeper. The real keeper is twelve.

Ten forty. Oil for thirty minutes at half wick, fifteen at full. The ferry is due at eleven. Then three things happen at once. In the window, a green light glides slowly to the left. From the west, a long, low horn. And out on the reef, the bell starts ringing again, fast, faster than you have ever heard it.

Maisie told you something on the stairs, hours ago, between a hundred other things. "The bell rings fast when something big pushes the water past it."

Freeze. Option one, full flame now, and follow the green light. Option two, wait until the horn sounds louder and closer. Option three, full flame now, because of the bell.

Pause the video. Decide now. Something big is very close.

You choose option three. You turn the wick all the way up. The beam blazes. Out in the fog, a huge pale shape swings hard away, its wake hissing over the rocks. The ferry. Two hundred metres from the reef.

The horn was the ferry too, but fog bends sound, and the wind dragged it west. And the green light? Look behind you. Your own signal lamp, green filter still on, sitting on the shelf, reflected in the glass. You were about to chase yourself.

The radio crackles. "Gull Rock, thank you," a voice says. "We nearly didn't see you."

Ten fifty-five. Oil for five minutes.

Then the torch in the seal cave flashes again, fast and urgent. High water is filling the cave. Amos has to row out now, in darkness, around the reef, to the slipway below you.

You open the black book to its last page. A drawing, in his father's careful hand. Two lanterns at the slipway, one high on the post, one low by the water. Under it, one line. "The big light shows the danger. Two lights show the way. Keep them in a line, and you are in the gap."

Freeze. Option one, keep the main beam blazing, so Amos can see the tower. Option two, flash the signal lamp at the cave, so he knows you are still watching. Option three, carry two lanterns down to the slipway and set them one above the other.

Pause the video. Decide now. His boat is already moving.

You choose option three. You and Maisie race down a hundred and twelve steps. One lantern on the post. One by the water. Behind you, high above, the main lamp coughs, flickers, and goes dark.

You wait in the wind.

Then, out of the fog, the creak of oars. Slow. Steady. And there he is, rowing straight through the gap in the reef, eyes fixed on two small lights in a line. The big beam would only have shown him the tower, and the tower stands behind the rocks. A lighthouse tells you where not to go. Only the lanterns could tell him where to go.

Amos climbs onto the slipway, soaked, grinning, and pulls Maisie into a hug so tight her raincoat squeaks.

At dawn, the coastguard boat noses into the harbour. Before anyone climbs the steps, you open the green log, and in your own handwriting, you write the truth. "Keeper's hands unsteady. Light kept for months by his granddaughter. Requesting a full-time assistant keeper. I volunteer."

Amos reads it twice. "You'd stay?" he said. "Out here?"

"Somebody has to keep the lanterns in a line," you said.

Maisie says nothing. She just takes your hand.

So think back. At the last freeze, with the main lamp dying, would you have walked away from the biggest light you had?

Three logbooks. One forgery. And finally, one true entry.

Next time, the fog is thicker and the light itself is lying. Subscribe, and keep your lantern lit.
