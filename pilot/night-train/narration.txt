Three passengers, five hours until sunrise, and one ticket that should not exist. By sunrise, you will know why its owner never threw it away.
The night train stops so gently you almost sleep through it. Then the engine dies, the lights blink amber, and the silence pours in like cold water. Somewhere ahead, in all that white, an avalanche has buried the track.
The conductor is Rosa Penhale, nineteen, first week on the job, her name badge still shiny. She joined because her grandad worked this line. She reaches dispatch a second before the signal fades. A crackly voice says, "Snowcat reaches you at sunrise. In this whiteout they can't see a thing. They'll drive the line and listen." Then nothing.
Rosa checks the panel. "Battery at sixty percent," she says. "Below ten, everything shuts off. Heaters, lights, all of it." Outside, it is minus eighteen.
There are only three other passengers. Mrs. Okafor, round and calm, knitting a scarf longer than she is. Theo, sixteen, cranking a little wind-up radio like a fishing reel. And Mr. Vane, old and quiet in a grey coat, who runs his thumb along the window frame and mutters, "Still haven't fixed this latch."
Rosa whispers one more thing you will need. "The heaters drink power. The lights barely sip."
Forty minutes pass. Battery at fifty-one percent. Frost creeps across the inside of the glass.

You choose option three. Five people, one small room, knees touching. Mrs. Okafor hands out wool like a magician. The drain slows to a crawl. Three heaters on low would have warmed empty seats and drained everything by four. Heat you don't share is heat you waste.
It works. For a while.
Then Rosa goes pale. "The roof marker lights run on the same battery," she says. "If we spend it all on warmth, there's nothing left to be seen with." Every percent of heat is a percent of being found. Four hours left. Battery at forty-four percent.
Rescue needs a passenger list, so Rosa checks tickets by torchlight. Mrs. Okafor, coach two, seat fourteen. Theo, student pass, coach three. Then Mr. Vane hands over a ticket that is soft and yellow, with an ink stamp instead of a barcode.
"Coach four, berth B," Rosa reads. This train has three coaches.
Nobody speaks. Mr. Vane just watches the frosted window.
Freeze. Option one, move Mr. Vane to the empty front coach until rescue, because a fake ticket might hide something worse. Option two, ignore it, because tickets don't matter tonight. Option three, ask him one question that only a real passenger of coach four could answer.
Pause the video. Decide now. Your clock keeps running.
You choose option three. "What was coach four?"
For the first time, he smiles. "The guard's van," he says. "It had an iron stove. When they cut the van off, they boarded the stove up in the back of coach three. Behind the luggage rack."
Remember the latch he knew was broken. A stranger doesn't know a train's old injuries. Locking him away would have locked away the only person who knew this train.
Behind a plywood panel there it is. A squat black stove and a sack of old coal. Theo finds a lighter. Ten minutes later the compartment glows orange, and you switch off the last heater. Battery holding at forty-one percent.
Mrs. Okafor laughs and wraps half of her endless scarf around Mr. Vane's shoulders.

Here is what you got wrong. He never lied. His ticket was simply older than the train.
Three in the morning. Snow piles higher. The windows go dark.
At four forty, three things happen at once. A sharp beep from the battery panel, flashing twenty percent. A long crack from the roof above your heads. And a soft, steady hiss from the stove pipe.
Earlier, when the fire caught, Mr. Vane had tapped that pipe. "If she ever hisses," he said, "the chimney is drowning in snow."
Freeze. Option one, the battery alarm. Shut down the marker lights now. Option two, the crack in the roof. Get everyone down onto the corridor floor. Option three, the hiss. Reach up through the roof hatch and push the snow cap off the chimney with Rosa's signal pole.
Pause the video. Decide now. The air is getting thick.
You choose option three. Rosa holds your boots while you jab the pole upward. A heavy slab of snow slides away, and the pipe breathes again. Grey smoke rises straight into the dark. "The panel cries wolf at twenty," Rosa admits. "The real cutoff is ten." And the crack? Just snow settling, the roof talking in its sleep.
Silence. Only the stove, ticking.
Five fifty. Sunrise in twenty minutes. Battery at nine percent. Theo hears it first. Far away, a low engine, grinding, fading in and out with the wind.
Rosa's hand hovers over the controls. "The horn runs on stored air," she says. "Three blasts. That's all we get."
Freeze. Option one, switch on every marker light now and burn the last nine percent. Option two, blow all three horn blasts right away, so they know early. Option three, wait until the engine is close and loud, then blow the horn three times.
Pause the video. Decide now. They might drive right past.
You choose option three. You wait. The engine grows louder. Louder. Now.
Three long blasts roll across the valley. The engine stops. Then it turns toward you.
Dispatch said it in the first minute. In a whiteout, they can't see. They drive the line and listen. Lights would have died unseen at dawn. Early blasts would have vanished in the wind.
The snowcat crew helps everyone out into a pink sunrise. Mr. Vane goes last. He presses his old ticket into Rosa's hand.
It is dated forty years ago tonight. Same train, same pass, same snow. He was a boy in coach four, and the guard lit the iron stove and told stories until morning.
"The guard's name," Mr. Vane said, "was Penhale."
Rosa stares down at her shiny badge. "That was my grandad," she said.
"I ride this train every year on this night," he said. "Just in case."
